\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Medical image segmentation models are trained on one acquisition setting and
are frequently applied to another: a different scanner, sequence, or imaging
modality than the one seen during training. MRI contrast is a protocol choice
rather than a physical constant, so the same anatomy yields incompatible
intensity distributions across sequences, and a network that has only seen
T1-weighted brain images can fail outright on T2-weighted or FLAIR images of
the same anatomy~\cite{billot2023synthseg,molinier2026auglab}. Acquiring and
annotating training data for every contrast a model might encounter is not
practical: manual segmentation is expensive, and clinical sites rarely share
raw data across institutions~\cite{liu2021feddg,wang2020dofe}. The
single-source domain generalization setting --- train on one labeled dataset
in one contrast, generalize to unseen contrasts and modalities without any
target-domain data --- is therefore the setting that matters for deployment,
and the one a growing body of work
addresses~\cite{zhang2020bigaug,ouyang2022gin,su2023slaug,xu2021randconv}.

The dominant single-source strategy is label-generative domain randomization,
popularized by SynthSeg~\cite{billot2023synthseg}. Given a \emph{dense}
anatomical label map --- a voxel-wise parcellation of the whole image, not the
sparse structure-of-interest annotation a segmentation dataset normally
provides --- intensities are resampled from a per-label Gaussian mixture,
producing images of arbitrary, unrealistic contrast that force the network to
ignore contrast and rely on shape and layout~\cite{billot2023synthseg}. This is
effective, but carries two structural costs. First, it is \emph{label-driven}:
the dense parcellation it resamples from is a richer annotation than the task
itself requires, so generating synthetic contrast costs exactly the expensive
labeling the method aims to reduce reliance on. Second, and less discussed, it
\emph{does not preserve texture}: replacing every labeled region with
piecewise Gaussian noise discards the real gradients, partial-volume
boundaries, and unlabelled fine structure the acquired scan contains. Layout
survives; texture does not.

We ask whether discarding texture is necessary for contrast robustness, or
only convenient once a dense label map is assumed. Our central finding is that
the answer depends on the \emph{task}: preserving real intensity structure
helps most where the target is defined by tissue appearance, and much less
where an anatomical interface separates it from its surroundings. Prior work shows that texture carries diagnostic signal in such
tasks: texture features separate MS lesions from normal-appearing white
matter~\cite{zhang2008mstexture}, and diffuse glioma infiltrates beyond every
margin visible on conventional MRI~\cite{gliomamargins2025,flairectomy2022}.
We show that this distinction determines where a texture-preserving
augmentation helps substantially.

We exploit it with \textbf{PALETTE-Aug}, a single-source augmentation that
needs no dense label map. PALETTE-Aug groups the foreground intensities of a
scan into classes with 1-D $k$-means, splits each class into spatial
sub-regions with a Voronoi partition, and applies a signed random affine remap
to the \emph{real} intensities of each sub-region. Because existing intensities
are remapped rather than replaced by noise, edges, gradients and partial-volume
detail survive inside each sub-region. Only the contrast between regions is
randomized, including sign inversions that no acquisition protocol produces.
An optional last step uses labels, and only the sparse task labels that any
segmentation dataset already has.

We test the mechanism by intervention. Keeping the partition and the
region target means, we replace only the real-intensity remap with
SynthSeg-style noise fill, in fourteen training settings, and measure the
change relative to the Dice the swap starts from. The largest relative effects
are all on targets without a tissue interface ($+44\%$ and $+18\%$ on brain
tumor, $+39\%$ on MS lesions, the last for all eight test patients), and no
interface-bounded setting gains more than $6\%$. The converse does not
hold: several appearance-defined settings, breast lesions among them, show no
effect, so we state the result in one direction only
(\S\ref{subsec:e-ablation}). A segmentation-free gradient measurement confirms
that the swap changes texture as intended: the noise-fill rungs keep only the
partition edges, and the real-fill swap restores the source texture.

We train one nnU-Net~\cite{isensee2021nnunet} configuration, varying only the
epoch budget per dataset, and evaluate cross-contrast, cross-modality and
cross-dataset transfer on eight tasks: healthy brain, MS lesion, brain tumor,
abdominal organ, mandible, breast lesion, spine and pelvis. We compare against
a no-augmentation baseline, two variants of SynthSeg (the released
dense-label variant and our re-implementation of the unreleased EM variant),
SRCSM~\cite{thaler2025semrandconv}, and Auglab~\cite{molinier2026auglab}, a
published cross-modality augmentation pipeline. PALETTE-Aug is the best method
overall: it has the best Dice on six of the eight tasks and is significantly
better than every competitor across tasks, on both Dice and boundary distance.
The best competitor switches between Auglab and SRCSM from task to task, while
PALETTE-Aug stays at or near the top throughout.

In summary, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A task-level account of when texture preservation matters.}
    Preserving real intensity structure helps most, relative to the model's
    starting point, where the segmentation target has no anatomical interface
    separating it from its surroundings. We test this with a controlled intervention rather than
    asserting it post hoc, and report where it does not extend.
    \item \textbf{A causal ablation isolating texture.} Swapping only the
    real-intensity remap for noise fill, with the partition fixed, places the
    largest relative effects on no-interface targets in fourteen training
    settings. A
    segmentation-free gradient measurement confirms that the swap changes
    texture as intended.
    \item \textbf{PALETTE-Aug}, the single-source contrast augmentation that
    exploits this, requiring no dense anatomical label map and using only the
    sparse task labels a segmentation dataset already provides.
    \item \textbf{Breadth evidence.} Across cross-contrast, cross-modality and
    cross-dataset transfer on eight tasks and sixteen datasets, with one
    configuration reused throughout (Spine was run on a separate pipeline),
    PALETTE-Aug is best on six tasks and
    significantly better than every competitor across tasks. Where it does not
    win we report whether the method ahead is actually ahead: on healthy brain
    it is a tie, on mandible a real loss.
\end{itemize}
