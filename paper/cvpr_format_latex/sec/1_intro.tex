\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Medical image segmentation models are often trained on one acquisition
setting~\cite{guan2022dasurvey} and frequently applied to another: a different
scanner, sequence, or imaging modality than the one seen during
training~\cite{ghafoorian2017transfer,karani2018lifelong}. MRI contrast is a
protocol choice, not a physical constant, so the same anatomy has different
intensities across sequences~\cite{bitar2006pulsesequences}. A network trained only on
T1-weighted brain images can fail outright on T2-weighted or FLAIR
images~\cite{billot2023synthseg}. Acquiring and
annotating training data for every contrast a model might encounter is not
practical: manual segmentation is expensive, and clinical sites rarely share
raw data across institutions~\cite{liu2021feddg,wang2020dofe}. The setting that matters for deployment is therefore single-source domain
generalization: train on one labeled dataset in one contrast, then generalize
to unseen contrasts and modalities without target-domain
data~\cite{zhang2020bigaug,ouyang2022gin,su2023slaug,xu2021randconv}.

The dominant single-source strategy is label-generative domain randomization,
popularized by SynthSeg~\cite{billot2023synthseg}. SynthSeg does not use the
real image at all: it builds each training image from a label map, filling
every labeled structure with Gaussian noise of a randomly drawn mean and
variance. The label map must be \emph{dense}, a voxel-wise parcellation of
the whole image rather than the sparse annotation a segmentation dataset
normally provides. The resulting images have arbitrary, unrealistic contrast,
forcing the network to rely on shape and layout~\cite{billot2023synthseg}.
This is effective, but carries two structural costs. First, it is
\emph{label-driven}: the dense parcellation it needs is richer than the task's
own annotation. SynthSeg trains on whole-brain parcellations with dozens of
labeled structures~\cite{billot2023synthseg}, whereas an MS dataset such as
Open-MS annotates only the lesions~\cite{lesjak2018openms}; obtaining the
parcellation reintroduces the expensive labeling the method aims to avoid. Second, and less discussed, it
\emph{does not preserve texture}: replacing every labeled region with
piecewise Gaussian noise discards the real gradients, partial-volume
boundaries, and unlabeled fine structure the acquired scan contains. Layout
survives; texture does not.

We ask when keeping texture, rather than discarding it, helps contrast
robustness. Our central finding is that
the answer depends on the \emph{task}: preserving real intensity structure
helps most where the target is defined by tissue appearance, and much less
where an anatomical interface separates it from its surroundings. Prior work shows that texture carries diagnostic signal in appearance-defined
tasks: texture features separate MS lesions from normal-appearing white
matter~\cite{zhang2008mstexture}, and diffuse glioma infiltrates beyond every
margin visible on conventional MRI~\cite{gliomamargins2025,flairectomy2022}.

\textbf{PALETTE-Aug}, our single-source augmentation, keeps this texture and
needs no dense label map. PALETTE-Aug groups the foreground intensities of a
scan into classes with 1-D $k$-means, splits each class into spatial
sub-regions with a Voronoi partition, and applies a signed random affine remap
to the \emph{real} intensities of each sub-region. Edges, gradients and partial-volume detail therefore survive inside each
sub-region, because existing intensities are remapped rather than replaced by
noise. Only the contrast between regions is randomized, and without a
target: PALETTE-Aug can produce any contrast, including implausible ones such
as inverted intensities, because it is not tied to a specific target contrast.
An optional step also re-randomizes each labeled structure; it uses whatever
labels exist, a dense label map or just the task's own segmentation labels.

We then test when texture matters, with a controlled ablation in sixteen
training settings. We keep PALETTE-Aug's partition and region target means and
change only what fills each region: the region's own image content, affinely
remapped so that its gradients and fine structure survive (texture kept), or
Gaussian noise (texture removed). We report the Dice of
real fill relative to that of noise fill. Pooled per task, the relative gain is
largest on the appearance-defined
targets, brain tumor ($+15\%$) and MS lesions ($+16\%$), against at most
$7\%$ on interface-bounded tasks. The converse does not hold: breast lesions,
also appearance-defined, gain only $1.4\%$, and several appearance-defined
settings show no gain, so we state the result in one direction only
(\S\ref{subsec:e-ablation}). A
segmentation-free gradient measurement confirms that the swap changes texture:
noise fill keeps only the partition edges, and real fill recovers most of the
source texture.

We train one nnU-Net~\cite{isensee2021nnunet} configuration, varying only the
epoch budget per dataset, and evaluate cross-contrast and cross-modality
transfer on eight tasks: healthy brain, MS lesion, brain tumor,
abdominal organ, mandible, breast lesion, spine and pelvis. We compare against
nnU-Net with its default augmentation; two variants of SynthSeg: SynthSeg-noEM,
the released SynthSeg, which its authors developed and tested on the brain, and
SynthSeg-EM, which first subdivides each label into sub-regions by
expectation-maximization (EM) clustering of the image intensities, a variant
the same authors developed and tested on cardiac
MRI~\cite[\S5.4]{billot2023synthseg} but whose code was not released when this
work was done, so we re-implemented it;
SRCSM~\cite{thaler2025semrandconv}; and Auglab~\cite{molinier2026auglab}, a
published cross-modality augmentation pipeline. PALETTE-Aug wins the benchmark
overall: it has the best Dice on six of the eight tasks and significantly beats
every competitor on five of them. Pooling all tasks into one test, it is
significantly better than each competitor on both Dice and boundary distance,
though not on every task.

In summary, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{When texture preservation matters, tested by intervention.}
    Replacing noise fill with the real-intensity remap, with the partition fixed,
    gives the largest gains relative to the starting Dice on appearance-defined
    targets, though not on every one. A segmentation-free gradient measurement
    confirms that the swap changes texture.
    \item \textbf{PALETTE-Aug}, a single-source contrast augmentation that
    needs no dense label map, only the task's own labels. In every comparison
    it runs as one added operator inside the Auglab pipeline; the ablation also
    tests it alone.
    \item \textbf{Breadth evidence.} Across cross-contrast and cross-modality
    transfer on eight tasks and sixteen datasets, with one
    configuration reused throughout, PALETTE-Aug wins the benchmark overall: it is best on six tasks and
    significantly beats every competitor on five; where it does not win, we
    test whether the leader is truly ahead.
\end{itemize}
