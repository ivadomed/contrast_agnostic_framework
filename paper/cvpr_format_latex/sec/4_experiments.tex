\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:e-setup}

\paragraph{Protocol.} We train a single real-intensity source modality per
dataset and evaluate on held-out modalities of the \emph{same} segmentation
task, using one fixed nnU-Net~\cite{isensee2021nnunet} configuration across
all settings. No target-domain data, of any modality, is seen during
training. Our full method, denoted \textbf{Ours} throughout, is PALETTE-Aug
applied as one additional contrast-augmentation operator inside the Auglab
pipeline~\cite{molinier2026auglab}; the \emph{Auglab} baseline is the same
pipeline with the PALETTE-Aug operator removed, so the two differ by exactly the
PALETTE-Aug transform. Every method is trained with the identical nnU-Net
schedule --- SGD with Nesterov momentum ($0.99$), the nnU-Net
\texttt{polyLR} decay from an initial rate of $10^{-2}$, $250$ mini-batches
per epoch, and the combined Dice-plus-cross-entropy loss --- and differs only
in the augmentation applied at the input. The epoch budget is fixed per
dataset and shared by every method on that dataset ($2500$ for Brats-GLI,
$2000$ for ON-Harmony and Open-MS, $1000$ for ToothFairy2 and I-SPY2, $200$
for CHAOS and Pelvis; the Spine models were trained on a separate pipeline with the same method
suite, \cref{sec:suppl-data}), and
each method is trained as three models on three train/validation folds and
all three are scored on the same held-out test set. All significance tests are
paired, use the patient as the unit, and are Holm-corrected~\cite{holm1979}; full test construction, configuration
details, and per-dataset descriptions (subject counts, acquisition
protocols, and label provenance) are given in
\cref{sec:suppl-persetting,sec:suppl-config,sec:suppl-data}.

\paragraph{Datasets.} We evaluate on eight tasks drawn from sixteen datasets
(\cref{tab:datasets}): abdominal organs, healthy brain, brain tumor, MS
lesions, mandible, breast lesions, spine and pelvic musculoskeletal anatomy,
across MRI, CT and dental CBCT. Brain tumor and MS lesions use
Brats2024-GLI~\cite{deverdier2024brats2024} and
Open-MS~\cite{lesjak2018openms}. Every task trains on a single source modality
at a time and is tested on every other contrast of that task \emph{and} the
training contrast itself --- eighteen source-modality settings in total; no
target modality is seen during training. Wherever an external cohort exists,
its contrasts are pooled into that task as further held-out test columns
rather than reported as a task of their own: CHAOS~\cite{kavur2021chaos}
transfers to CT from AMOS~\cite{ji2022amos} and
SLIVER07~\cite{heimann2009comparison}; Mandible
(ToothFairy2~\cite{bolelli2025toothfairy2}, CBCT only) to the CT and MR of
HaN-Seg~\cite{podobnik2023hanseg} and the CT of PDDCA~\cite{raudaschl2017pddca},
which makes it the one task whose held-out axis is entirely cross-dataset;
Breast (I-SPY2~\cite{li2022ispy2}) to
Duke-Breast-MRI~\cite{saha2018duke,garrucho2025mamamia},
I-SPY1~\cite{hylton2012ispy1} and ACRIN-6698~\cite{partridge2018acrin6698},
the last contributing diffusion-weighted MRI, a contrast neither training
modality resembles; and Spine (a CT/Dixon cohort) to the T1w and T2w of
SPIDER~\cite{vandergraaf2024spider}. Pelvis is built from the TotalSegmentator CT and MRI
releases~\cite{wasserthal2023totalseg,dantonoli2025totalsegmri}, two unpaired
cohorts. ON-Harmony~\cite{warrington2025onharmony} reference labels are
silver-standard, obtained on T1w with FreeSurfer~\cite{fischl2012freesurfer}
and registered to the other contrasts.

\paragraph{Baselines.} SynthSeg comes in two variants, which we refer to
throughout by how they obtain their per-class intensity model: \emph{noEM}, the
released variant, which reads it off a dense anatomical label map; and
\emph{EM}, which estimates it from the image by expectation-maximization and
therefore needs no dense parcellation. We compare against five methods. (1)
\emph{No-augmentation}, the nnU-Net default training recipe. (2)
\emph{SynthSeg-noEM}~\cite{billot2023synthseg}, the original
label-generative variant (the implementation released by the authors), which
synthesises each training image from a dense anatomical label map alone,
ignoring the acquired scan entirely. (3) \emph{SynthSeg-EM}, the variant described in
the original work but never released publicly, which we re-implement; unlike
SynthSeg-noEM it does not require a dense anatomical label map, but it is not
dense-label-free either --- it is compatible with and relies on the sparse task
labels naturally available in a segmentation dataset, which it uses to
estimate its per-class intensity model. (4) \emph{SRCSM}, a semantic random-convolution
contrast-synthesis augmentation, included as a second strong
synthesis-based competitor that is neither label-generative nor a
member of the Auglab family. (5) \emph{Auglab}, the published
GPU-accelerated augmentation pipeline of Molinier
et~al.~\cite{molinier2026auglab}. Auglab is a prior method in its own right,
not an ablation of ours, and we report it among the competitors accordingly;
our full method (\cref{subsec:e-setup}) applies PALETTE-Aug \emph{on top of} the
Auglab pipeline, so the PALETTE-vs-Auglab comparison also functions as the
add-one-component test isolating the contribution of the PALETTE-Aug transform.

\paragraph{PALETTE-Aug is the best method overall.} It has the best Dice on six
of the eight tasks. Across tasks it is significantly better than every
competitor ($p<10^{-10}$ each, \cref{tab:meta}). Within single tasks it
significantly outperforms every competitor on four tasks (Brats-GLI, Open-MS,
Breast, Spine), three or four of five on CHAOS, Mandible and Pelvis, and two
of five on ON-Harmony. The cross-task test
treats our eight tasks as fixed. Treating each task as one unit instead, which
is the question of how a method fares on a new task, PALETTE-Aug beats Auglab on
seven of eight tasks (one-sided sign test $p=0.035$) and SRCSM on five of the
seven tasks where SRCSM was run ($p=0.23$).

SynthSeg-noEM samples intensities from a dense anatomical label map. It works
only where such maps exist and falls below the no-augmentation baseline on six
of the eight tasks. SynthSeg-EM, which estimates its intensity model from the
task labels, closes most of that gap. SRCSM also falls below the baseline on
Breast.

\paragraph{No competitor is reliably second.} The best competitor alternates
between Auglab (Brats-GLI, Open-MS, Breast, Spine) and SRCSM (CHAOS,
ON-Harmony, Mandible, Pelvis), and SRCSM fails on Breast ($16.4$ Dice).
Picking the right one would require knowing the answer in advance.
PALETTE-Aug is first or second on every task but one.

\paragraph{Where PALETTE-Aug does not win.} The one-sided test above cannot show
that a competitor is ahead, so on these tasks we also ran the reverse test on
the same patient-level design (Holm-corrected across competitors within the
task). On ON-Harmony SRCSM leads by $0.1$ Dice and neither method is
significantly ahead. On Mandible SRCSM ($+1.3$ Dice, $p=6\times10^{-4}$) and
Auglab ($+0.6$, $p=2.5\times10^{-3}$) are significantly ahead, and PALETTE-Aug
is third. In the Mandible ablation the texture step is significantly positive,
and the drop coincides with adding the Auglab pipeline on top
(\cref{tab:ladder-full}).

\paragraph{Boundary distance.} HD95 is reported in the supplement
(\cref{tab:meta-hd95}). PALETTE-Aug has the lowest HD95 on five of the eight
tasks (Brats-GLI, ON-Harmony, Open-MS, Spine, Pelvis) and is significantly
better than every competitor across tasks. It ties Auglab on Breast, trails
SRCSM on CHAOS without a significant gap, and is significantly behind SRCSM,
Auglab and the baseline on Mandible.

\subsection{Causal ablation: when does texture matter?}
\label{subsec:e-ablation}

PALETTE-Aug's partition could help on its own, without the texture-preserving
remap. We test this with a ladder in which each rung adds one ingredient:
baseline $\rightarrow$ $+k$-means $\rightarrow$ $+$label remap $\rightarrow$
$+$Voronoi $\rightarrow$ real-intensity fill $\rightarrow$ $+$Auglab. The three
partition rungs fill every region with label-style Gaussian noise, as SynthSeg
does. All rungs run the same pipeline (\cref{sec:method}) in the same order and
only switch steps on. The step from $+$Voronoi to real fill changes only the
fill: the partition, the number of sub-regions, the distribution of region
target means and both blur passes are the same on each side. A change at that step is therefore
due to preserving the real texture.

\cref{fig:ladder,tab:dissociation} support a one-directional result. Every
large gain from real texture is on a target defined by tissue appearance
rather than by an anatomical interface: Brats-GLI T1c ($+13.3$ Dice) and T1n
($+7.2$), both significant after correction over the fourteen rows, and
Open-MS FLAIR ($+7.7$), which improves for all eight test patients but cannot
reach significance with eight. No interface row gains more than $1.4$; CHAOS
T1in and ToothFairy2 gain about one point. Pooled per task
(\cref{fig:ladder}), the step is worth $+5.3$ on Brats-GLI and $+4.2$ on
Open-MS, against $+1.0$ on CHAOS, $+1.4$ on ToothFairy2 and $-1.9$ on
ON-Harmony. The converse does not hold. Several appearance-defined settings
show no Dice effect: Open-MS T1w, Brats-GLI T2w and FLAIR, and both Breast rows
($-0.85$ and $+0.50$, four cohorts pooled by contrast). So a large texture
gain indicates an appearance-defined target, but an appearance-defined target
does not guarantee one.

ON-Harmony labels 31 brain regions with the standard FreeSurfer subcortical
parcellation~\cite{fischl2012freesurfer}: ventricles, cortex, white matter,
cerebellum, brainstem and eight deep grey nuclei. Every one is bounded by a
tissue-type transition (CSF against tissue, grey against white matter), so we
group it with CHAOS as an interface task. Its negative effect is larger than
CHAOS's, perhaps because it labels nearly the whole brain; one densely labeled
task cannot test this. Its reference labels are also made on T1w and
registered to the other contrasts, which may favor some fills over others.

\paragraph{Does the fill swap change texture? Noise versus real fill.}
The ladder shows that the fill swap changes Dice, not that it changes texture.
We check this without any segmentation, with the normalized-gradient-field
(NGF) similarity~\cite{haber2006ngf} between each augmented volume and its
source scan. NGF measures how well image gradients align: it is high when
edges and fine structure are kept and falls to about $1/3$ for isotropic
noise. \Cref{fig:ngf-dose} follows NGF and Dice along the same rungs on Open-MS
FLAIR. The three noise-fill rungs stay at NGF $0.45$--$0.46$, above the noise
floor because the partition edges survive but far below the source, while their
Dice rises from $11.2$ to $19.6$. Those gains come from contrast randomization,
not texture. Only the real-fill swap raises both, NGF to $0.81$ and Dice to
$27.3$. \Cref{tab:ngf} compares the methods on the same scale. PALETTE-Aug
(the real-fill rung, without Auglab) keeps significantly more source texture
than Auglab (paired Wilcoxon over the $30$ scans; FLAIR $p=3.2\times10^{-3}$,
T1w $p=1.2\times10^{-6}$), and the label-style methods stay near the floor.

\paragraph{Why Auglab is the closest competitor.} Auglab is the strongest
competitor on the cross-task test, and NGF suggests why: it keeps most of the
source texture, only modestly less than PALETTE-Aug and far more than the
label-style methods (\cref{tab:ngf}). The large margins appear against the
methods that discard texture.

\paragraph{What the test does and does not show.} It compares two versions of
one method that differ in one component, so differences in augmentation
strength, partition granularity or training schedule cannot explain it. It is
also separate from the benchmark ranking: on Mandible the texture step helps
while the full method places third (\S\ref{subsec:e-main}).
