\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A possible reason for the largest effects.} Liver, spleen and kidney are
encapsulated organs with a clear physical boundary, and edge-following methods
have long segmented them well~\cite{lee2007gaclivers}. An
augmentation that scrambles their internal texture but keeps the boundary loses
little. Diffuse glioma has no such boundary: tumor cells infiltrate beyond both
the enhancing margin and the FLAIR abnormality~\cite{gliomamargins2025,flairectomy2022},
so the target must be inferred from appearance. MS lesions, though typically
well demarcated, are identified by abnormal signal within white matter, and
texture features separate them from normal-appearing white
matter~\cite{zhang2008mstexture}. This account interprets the intervention of
\cref{subsec:e-ablation}; it is not independent evidence.

\paragraph{Not every appearance-defined target gains much.} Glioma T2w and
FLAIR show no significant Dice gain, and Breast gains only slightly:
$+2.1$ Dice from T2w training but $-1.2$ from T1-CE. We have two hypotheses. First, the effect depends sharply on the
training contrast (Glioma: $+12.7$ from T1c, $+6.0$ from T1n, $+1.1$ and
$-1.3$ from T2w and FLAIR), so whether its texture transfers may matter as much as the
target; with T2w training, real fill helps some labels and hurts others
(\cref{tab:per-label-delta}). Second, Breast pools four cohorts from different
trials, which may dilute a cohort-specific effect. On interface-bounded
tasks, where texture adds little relative to the starting Dice, most of the
gain arrives with the texture-free partition rungs (\cref{tab:ladder-full}),
that is, from the wide range of region-to-region contrasts PALETTE-Aug
samples.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We assign each target to a group by its anatomy, not
by a measured boundary statistic, and the grouping is ours. Target type is
confounded with difficulty: all interface-bounded rows but one start above
$63\%$ noise-fill Dice and every appearance-defined row below $42\%$. We
therefore lead with the absolute Dice change; dividing by the starting Dice
favors the appearance-defined rows, and as a share of the remaining error
($100\%$ minus noise-fill Dice) Abdomen T2spir would rank first. The
exception, Pelvis CT, starts at $35\%$ and gains $+4.8$ (not significant),
below the four large appearance-defined gains.
The between-group comparison is descriptive: a permutation test on the
seven per-task gains gives $p=0.2$. MS has eight test patients and Brain eight
sessions of four subjects, so their single-setting effects can be consistent
without reaching significance, and
Brain's session-level tests are optimistic. Spine was run on a separate
pipeline and has no SRCSM arm. The cross-task test covers new patients, not new tasks; with the task as the
unit, the advantage over SRCSM is not significant.
We applied each competitor as its paper proposes and did not develop it
further. In particular, we did not combine SRCSM or SynthSeg with the full
Auglab pipeline, which would be a new method of our own design; part of
PALETTE-Aug's margin over them may therefore come from Auglab, which only the
comparison with Auglab itself controls. We also left out SRCSM's test-time
histogram matching, so its Breast score may be lower than its full pipeline
would give. PALETTE-Aug targets the single-source setting and is complementary to
multi-source domain generalization, which assumes several labeled source
domains.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked when preserving real image texture matters for contrast-robust
segmentation. In our sixteen settings, every large gain from keeping texture,
$+5.9$ to $+12.7$ Dice, is on a target defined by tissue appearance (brain
tumor, MS lesions), against at most $+4.8$ where an anatomical interface
bounds the target; not every appearance-defined target gains. PALETTE-Aug,
which preserves texture and needs no dense label map, wins the eight-task
benchmark overall and significantly beats every competitor on five tasks.
Whether the pattern holds on new tasks, and whether a measured boundary
statistic can replace our anatomical grouping, remains to be tested.
