\section{Statistical testing}
\label{sec:suppl-stats}

\paragraph{Test construction.} All tests are paired, and the
unit of independence is the \emph{patient}, never the fold, the image or the
contrast. A patient's score is its mean over labels and evaluated folds; where
the same patient is scored in several held-out contrasts, or by the models of
several training modalities, those scores move together as one unit under the
null. Treating them as separate samples would be pseudo-replication and would
inflate the effective sample size --- a real risk here, since several tasks
test a handful of patients under many contrasts (Brain: eight sessions
of four subjects, six contrasts). For Brain the unit is the session, since
its four subjects leave a test that could never reach significance; two
sessions of one subject are not fully independent, so its $p$-values are, if
anything, optimistic, and we state Brain-only results with this caveat.
Per-setting comparisons (\cref{sec:suppl-persetting}) use the same test within
each setting. The ablation ladders use the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank
test~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, since there each setting is judged on its own; each
patient then contributes one difference, its mean over the held-out contrasts
it appears in, so patients, not contrasts, are weighted equally. All tests are
Holm-corrected~\cite{holm1979} for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Why the aggregate tests are sign-flip tests on a macro-average.}
The comparisons between methods (\cref{tab:meta,tab:meta-hd95,tab:suppl-dice,tab:suppl-hd95})
are not paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, which is the more usual choice in
this literature: SynthSeg+, from the lineage we compare against, marks a
method as better than its competitors using a one-sided Bonferroni-corrected
Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired Dice~\cite{billot2023synthsegplus}. We
therefore give our reason rather than leave it implicit. The \emph{estimand}
we test, by contrast, is not unusual at all --- it is the same
equal-weight-per-stratum principle the Medical Segmentation Decathlon
adopted, for the same reason, as set out below.

\emph{The estimand.} What we claim is $\text{macro}\Delta$: the mean, over
test contrasts, of that contrast's mean paired per-case difference
(ours minus competitor) --- equal weight per contrast, which is exactly what
every task column of \cref{tab:meta} and every setting column of
\cref{tab:suppl-dice} reports, so the test and the table measure the same
thing. Equal weighting is not cosmetic here. The claim
under test is cross-contrast generalization, and a contrast's held-out case
count reflects how large a public cohort happens to be, not how much that
contrast should say about generalizing. Abdomen is the extreme case: its CT
column pools $140$ cases ($20$ from CHAOS, $100$ from AMOS, $20$ from
SLIVER07), while CHAOS's three MR contrasts share the same $4$ patients and
AMOS T2w has $20$. Weighting by case count would let CT cast most of the votes
in a test whose conclusion is about transfer across contrasts.

This is the same problem, and the same answer, as the Medical Segmentation
Decathlon's. Facing sample sizes that ``varied heavily across all tasks and
target ROIs,'' it ranked algorithms within each target ROI, averaged those
ranks per task, and averaged the per-task ranks for the final score ---
choosing that over a flat average across ROIs precisely because the flat
scheme means ``tasks with multiple target ROIs \ldots{} would be
over-weighted''~\cite{antonelli2022decathlon}. Our $\text{macro}\Delta$
applies the same principle one level across, with the held-out contrast in
place of the target ROI. Where we differ is only in how that weighted
quantity is reached: the Decathlon arrives at it through counts of pairwise
Wilcoxon outcomes at an uncorrected per-comparison $\alpha$, whereas we test
the weighted difference itself and Holm-correct across competitors.

\emph{Why not a pooled Wilcoxon.} The signed-rank test is defined on a
single undifferentiated sample of paired differences. Applying it to the
concatenation of all contrasts therefore tests a case-weighted null --- the
imbalance above, reintroduced --- and there is no standard reweighting of
the signed-rank statistic that recovers the equal-per-contrast estimand.
The disagreement this causes runs in both directions: a pooled test can
declare a tenth of a Dice point significant because one large cohort supplies
most of the cases, or miss a consistent effect confined to the smaller
contrasts.

\emph{The test.} Under the null that the two methods are exchangeable on a
given patient, that patient's paired differences are equally likely to carry
either sign. Sign-flipping is therefore a valid randomization null for \emph{any}
statistic computed from those differences~\cite{ernst2004permutation},
$\text{macro}\Delta$ included, which is what makes it applicable where the
signed-rank test is not. We
report the one-sided $p$ in the direction of the stated hypothesis.

\emph{Evaluating the tail.} The statistic is a weighted sum of per-patient
contributions, so its sign-flip null distribution can be computed rather than
sampled. We enumerate all $2^{M}$ sign patterns of the $M{=}20$ patients with
the largest contributions exactly, and treat the remaining, lighter patients as
a Gaussian term with their exact null variance; with twenty patients or fewer
the test is therefore exact. A plain normal approximation to the whole null,
which an earlier version of this analysis used, is badly conservative in
exactly the designs we have, where a few patients spanning several contrasts
carry most of the null variance: on the Abdomen table it returned $p=0.013$ for
a comparison that a $200{,}000$-draw Monte-Carlo test places below
$5\times10^{-6}$. The enumerated tail is deterministic and reaches far tails
without the $1/B$ floor of resampling. Its corollary is a hard floor for small
cohorts: eight patients admit $2^8=256$ sign patterns, so no one-sided $p$
can fall below $1/256$ however consistent the effect.

\paragraph{Contrast-group pooling.} For Abdomen, the same tested modality is
sometimes evidenced by more than one source: Abdomen's CT evidence comes from
CHAOS's own held-out CT split plus AMOS and SLIVER07. Averaging every raw
column with equal weight, the natural first implementation, silently
lets a modality with more contributing sources outvote one with fewer in
both the task-level average and the significance test, since it
then supplies more of the equally-weighted columns being averaged. We
instead pool disjoint sources of the \emph{same} modality by concatenating
their cases into one group before any averaging happens, then average
modality groups with equal weight. For Abdomen this additionally means
averaging equal-weight across liver/spleen/kidney \emph{within} the pooled
CT evidence, since AMOS's own CT column already blends all three organs
into one number before it reaches this step. The same pooling applies to
Mandible (HaN-Seg and PDDCA both supply CT) and Breast (I-SPY2, Duke
and I-SPY1 supply T1-CE, and Duke and I-SPY1 also supply
pre-contrast); the remaining tasks have no
contrast evidenced by more than one source.

\paragraph{Boundary distance at task level.} \Cref{tab:meta-hd95} gives the
HD95 counterpart of \cref{tab:meta}. Across tasks PALETTE-Aug is significantly
better than every competitor, Auglab included. Per task it has the lowest HD95
on Glioma, Abdomen, Brain, MS, Spine and Pelvis (on Abdomen the gap to
SRCSM is not significant); it ties Auglab on Breast without a significant gap; and on Mandible it
is significantly behind SRCSM, Auglab and the baseline, the
one task where the baseline beats PALETTE-Aug. PALETTE alone, shown for
reference, has the lowest HD95 on Breast. When HD95 disagrees with Dice for a
method (a noticeably better HD95 than its Dice would predict), the usual
explanation is low recall: a method that predicts only a small, confident,
well-localized fragment of the structure can have a deceptively good boundary
distance while missing most of the target, so a strong HD95 paired with a weak
Dice is a warning sign rather than evidence of competitiveness.

\paragraph{Full ladder: numbers and significance.} \Cref{fig:ladder} (main
paper) shows the rung sequence visually; \cref{tab:ladder-full} gives the
underlying out-of-domain Dice at every rung of all sixteen ladders, with
significance for each step against the rung immediately before it
(Holm-corrected paired Wilcoxon on patients, within each ladder's family of
steps). Most steps that add an ingredient help, as expected. Eight cells are
significant \emph{decreases} (marked $\downarrow$). Two are the fill-swap step
on Brain T1w and T2w. Two are the Voronoi sub-parcellation step, on
Glioma T1n and Abdomen T1in, which the real-fill step then more than
recovers. Three are the $+$Auglab rung --- on Brain DWI, Abdomen T1in and
Mandible, the task where the complete method places third
(\cref{subsec:e-main}) --- and one is Mandible's step to synthetic-contrast
validation. On Mandible, PALETTE alone (the real-fill rung, $69.5$) is the
ladder's maximum and adding the Auglab recipe takes it back down; adding
PALETTE to Auglab also lowers Mandible Dice (\cref{tab:meta}), so the two
components interact negatively there and we cannot attribute the shortfall to
either; we have not diagnosed which Auglab operator is responsible. Both
Abdomen ladders also peak at the real-fill rung.

We likewise do not hide that Voronoi's own marginal contribution is
task-dependent rather than uniformly positive. A separate paired
significance check on all-contrast Dice (the estimand of \cref{tab:mechanism},
not the OOD slice above) shows that Voronoi
significantly \emph{helps} MS FLAIR and (by a small margin) Brain
T1w, is flat on Abdomen T1in (its OOD step above is a small significant
decrease), and significantly \emph{hurts} Glioma T1n. The full method is still best on Glioma T1n
(\cref{tab:suppl-dice}; not significantly ahead of Auglab): whatever Voronoi costs there is more than recovered by
the subsequent real-intensity fill step, so the net effect of the complete
pipeline is positive even though one internal component is not.

One candidate mechanism, flagged for future work rather than tested here:
a texture pattern learned from one contrast's rendering of a diffuse,
texture-defined label like edema may not simply fail to transfer to a
different contrast's rendering of the same tissue; it may actively
mislead, if the model has come to rely on a contrast-specific cue that the
other contrast does not reproduce. This would be an instance of shortcut
learning~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}: a network relying on a
domain-specific cue that does not reproduce across acquisition protocols,
the mechanism~\citet{ouyang2022gin} identify behind failed
single-source domain generalization in cross-modality and cross-sequence
medical segmentation, rather than on the intended, causal feature, which a
simplicity-biased network need not prefer even when it would generalize
better~\cite{shah2020simplicity}. We have not tested this mechanistically
here; it is the most concrete hypothesis consistent with the label-specific,
direction-dependent pattern above, not a demonstrated cause.
